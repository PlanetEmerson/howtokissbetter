How Long Does It Take for a Hickey to Form? The Timeline

Twenty to thirty seconds of suction, then a five to ten minute wait. How long it takes for a hickey to form, how long it lasts, and what changes both.

The strangest thing about a hickey is the delay.

You stop. You check. Nothing is there. Smooth skin, no evidence, and for about five glorious minutes you get to believe you were careful.

Then it arrives.

That gap between the suction and the mark is why this question gets typed into a search bar at one in the morning, usually by somebody with a family dinner on Sunday. Here is the real clock: how many seconds it takes, why the bruise waits before it shows up, how long it sticks around afterward, and the one thing about hickey timelines that nearly every article states with far more certainty than the evidence allows.

The short answer

About 20 to 30 seconds of steady, unbroken suction will mark most people. The bruise usually surfaces 5 to 10 minutes after you stop, darkens over the following 12 to 24 hours, and then fades over roughly five days to two weeks.

Treat those numbers as a starting point rather than a promise. Some people mark in ten seconds. Others can be worked on for a solid minute and walk away with nothing but a good memory and an unblemished neck.

What is happening under the skin

A hickey is a bruise with an unusual origin story.

Ordinary bruises come from impact. Something hits you, small vessels rupture, blood leaks into the tissue around them. A hickey skips the impact entirely and runs the pressure the other way. A sealed mouth pulls against the skin, and that suction draws blood toward the surface faster than the capillaries can handle. They give out. Blood escapes into the surrounding tissue in tiny dots that cluster and merge into one visible patch.

What you end up looking at is not sitting on the skin. It is under it, showing through. That single fact explains why you cannot scrub a hickey off, and why every remedy that treats it like a stain is wasting your afternoon.

The seal is the engine of the whole thing. Lips happen to be very good at making one, which is the least romantic consequence of the dense muscle and nerve structure that makes them so ridiculously sensitive in the first place. Same hardware, different job.

The suction clock: how many seconds it takes

The working number is 20 to 30 seconds, and the important word in that sentence is not "seconds." It is "unbroken."

Suction only accumulates while the seal holds. Break it at twelve seconds to admire your progress, reseal, and you have not banked twelve seconds toward a total. You have mostly started over. The pressure difference is what moves blood, and the pressure difference dies the instant air gets in.

This is the single most common reason nothing shows up. Most people work in short bursts: five seconds, a look, five more seconds, another look. That is a great deal of enthusiasm producing a slightly pink neck and nothing else.

Force is the variable people reach for, and it is the wrong one. Harder suction mostly produces pain and a sharp-edged mark that reads less like affection and more like an industrial accident. Steady and sustained beats hard and brief every time. Why the mark waits until after you stop

Here is the part that catches everybody out.

The vessels break during the suction. The bruise appears several minutes later. Those are two separate events, and the distance between them is exactly why people overshoot.

Blood that has left a capillary has to spread through the tissue and pool close enough to the surface before it can be seen through skin at all. That spreading takes time. Then the escaped blood loses oxygen, the color deepens, and the mark keeps getting more obvious for the next 12 to 24 hours.

The practical consequence matters more than the biology. You cannot judge a hickey in the moment. The faint pink smudge at minute two is not the finished product; it is a trailer for it, and the finished product is darker and larger.

Anyone who has ever thought "that is barely anything" at midnight and then met a bathroom mirror at eight the next morning understands this on a personal level.

Why some people mark in ten seconds and others never do

Same mouth, same duration, wildly different results. The variables that actually move the number:

  • Skin thickness at the spot. Thin skin over shallow vessels marks fast. The side of the neck, the inner arm, and the hollow above the collarbone are all easy. A forearm or a thigh takes considerably more work.
  • Where the capillaries sit. Some people simply have more fragile vessels closer to the surface. It is not a flaw and it is not a talent. It is plumbing.
  • Age. Skin and vessel walls grow more fragile over time, which is why suction that leaves nothing on a twenty-five-year-old can leave a serious mark on someone thirty years older.
  • Blood thinners. Aspirin and prescribed anticoagulants make bleeding under the skin easier, and the resulting mark bigger and slower to clear.
  • Complexion. The bruise forms identically on every skin tone, but it does not read identically. On deeper skin tones a fresh hickey often looks brown, slate, or deep plum rather than red, and it can be genuinely hard to see in the first hour. That is a visibility difference, not a severity difference, and misreading it is the fastest route to overdoing it.

One honest aside before the fun resumes. If somebody bruises from almost no pressure, turns up with marks in places nobody touched, or needs weeks to clear something small, that belongs in a conversation with a doctor rather than a kissing blog. Easy bruising is often nothing at all. It is still worth a question.

How long a hickey lasts

Most hickeys fade somewhere between five days and two weeks. Small, light ones can be gone in three or four days. A determined one on thin skin can push past the two-week mark.

The chart everybody repeats

Search this question and the same tidy progression comes back every time. Red or purple on days one and two. Darker blue by day three. Green somewhere around days four to seven. Yellow just before it disappears. The colors have a real biological cause: hemoglobin in the escaped blood breaks down into biliverdin, which is green, and then bilirubin, which is yellow.

The biology is sound. The calendar bolted onto it is not.

The problem with the chart

Two pathologists at Cambridge went looking for that timetable and largely failed to find it. Langlois and Gresham gathered 369 photographs of bruises in 89 people aged 10 to 100, ranging from under six hours old to 21 days old, specifically to test whether a bruise could be dated by its appearance. Their study of how bruises change color with time found that yellow was the only genuinely meaningful signal, that it developed faster in people under 65, and that red did not change significantly with time at all.

Their conclusion ran to one sentence and was far humbler than the internet's: a bruise showing yellow is more than 18 hours old. That was the whole reliable finding.

A later review of the science of dating bruises by Langlois landed in a similar place. Yellow indicates a bruise is not recent, and past that point visual judgment runs straight into the limits of the human eye.

Use the color chart as a rough map of the journey. Do not use it as a schedule, and do not use it to date somebody's hickey to the hour, because the people who studied this hardest say you cannot. Where hickeys form fastest

Thin skin and shallow vessels win. Roughly in descending order of speed: the side and front of the neck, the hollow above the collarbone, the inner arm, the inner thigh. Thick skin over muscle is slow going.

The neck is the classic address because it is both the fastest and the most reachable, which is also precisely why it is the hardest one to explain on a Monday. Nothing about a mark under a shirt collar forms more slowly. It simply arrives with fewer follow-up questions.

How to tell in the moment that you have gone far enough

Three signals and one rule.

  • Break the seal and watch the skin. Flushed pink that fades within a few seconds means you are early. Flushed skin that stays flushed means the bruise is already committed.
  • Listen for the flinch. A hickey should register as pressure, not pain. Sharp pain means too much force, and force was never the variable that mattered.
  • Trust the clock over your instinct. Twenty seconds feels like nothing while it is happening and is usually plenty.

The rule: stop at the first faint sign, because what you can see is roughly half of what you are going to get.

And the part that comes before all of it. A hickey outlasts the mood that produced it, and it turns up in other people's photographs. Ask first. "Can I leave a mark?" takes one second and is the entire difference between a private souvenir and a problem somebody else has to solve with a scarf for a week.

When the mark is not a hickey

Suction bruises are ordinary and they behave like bruises. A few things deserve a second look rather than a shrug: a mark that appeared with no suction involved, swelling or heat around it, a mark that spreads outward over days instead of fading, or one still obvious after two or three weeks.

None of those automatically mean something is wrong. All of them are reasonable to raise with a doctor rather than a search engine.

The bottom line

Twenty to thirty seconds of unbroken suction. Five to ten minutes before anything appears. Twelve to twenty-four hours until it is as dark as it will get. Then somewhere between five days and two weeks of fading, on a timetable nobody can predict precisely, including the forensic researchers who tried hardest to.

The delay is the whole trap. You are never looking at the finished mark while you can still do something about it, which is why the only dependable approach is to stop earlier than feels necessary and to ask before you start. Frequently Asked Questions

How long does it take for a hickey to form?

About 20 to 30 seconds of steady, unbroken suction is enough for most people. The mark itself usually becomes visible 5 to 10 minutes after you stop, then keeps darkening for the next 12 to 24 hours.

Can a hickey form instantly?

No. The vessels break during the suction, but the escaped blood needs several minutes to spread and pool close enough to the surface to show through. Anything visible immediately is usually just flushed skin, and that fades.

How long do you have to suck to give a hickey?

Twenty to thirty seconds of continuous contact is the working range. Duration matters far more than force, and breaking the seal to check your progress resets most of the pressure you had built.

How long do hickeys last?

Most fade within five days to two weeks. Light ones can clear in three or four days. Larger marks, bruises on thin skin, and bruises on people taking blood thinners all take longer.

Why did my hickey not show up?

Skin thickness, vessel depth, age, and how consistently the seal held all change the result. Short bursts of suction with breaks in between are the most common reason no mark appears at all.

Can you tell how old a hickey is from its color?

Not reliably. Forensic research on bruise aging found that the only dependable color-based conclusion is that a bruise showing yellow is more than 18 hours old. Red in particular does not track with time.

Do hickeys get darker after the first day?

They usually deepen over the first 12 to 24 hours and then begin to fade. A mark that looks mild at bedtime often looks considerably more obvious the next morning.

Does getting a hickey hurt?

It should register as pressure and suction rather than pain. Sharp or lingering pain means the force is too high, and higher force does not produce a better mark.
