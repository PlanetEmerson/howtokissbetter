Can You Kiss Someone With a Cold? (And When to Skip the Kiss)

A kiss is a surprisingly poor way to catch a cold. Flu, strep, mono and cold sores play by other rules. What each means for kissing and how long to wait.

The Short Answer

Usually, yes, if they're on the mend and you both accept a small risk. A kiss is a surprisingly poor way to pass a common cold: in a 1984 study, only 1 of 13 healthy volunteers caught one after kissing an infected volunteer, while hand contact spread the same kind of virus far more efficiently. Other bugs play by other rules. Skip lip kisses with flu or COVID until 24 hours of improving symptoms with no fever, with strep until there's no fever and 12 to 24 hours of antibiotics, with mono until a doctor says otherwise, and with a cold sore until it has completely healed.

They've got a tissue up one sleeve, a voice like a foghorn and a mug of tea going cold on the nightstand. And they're leaning in.

You have about one second to decide whether this kiss is romance or a biohazard.

Here's what nobody tells you: if the bug is an ordinary cold, the kiss is one of the least efficient ways to catch it. In lab experiments, cold viruses moved far more easily through hands than through lips. So can you kiss someone with a cold? Usually, yes, with a few honest caveats. But "sick" covers a lot of ground, and flu, strep, mono and cold sores play by very different rules.

Below: what the actual experiments found, the bug-by-bug rules for when to skip the kiss, and how to show someone you adore them while they're shedding germs like confetti.

Can You Kiss Someone With a Cold? The Short Answer

Yes, most of the time, as long as you both accept a small risk. A common cold is surprisingly hard to pass mouth to mouth: in one study, just 1 of 13 healthy volunteers caught a cold after kissing an infected volunteer. Hands, and the air you share at kissing distance, are the bigger risks.

The answer changes with the bug:

  • A common cold: a kiss is a modest risk. Wait until they're on the mend if you can, and wash your hands more often than you kiss.
  • Flu or COVID: skip it while they're sick. The CDC's bar for normal activities is 24 hours of improving symptoms with no fever (and no fever reducers), then extra care for five more days.
  • Strep throat: skip it until the fever is gone and they've had antibiotics for at least 12 to 24 hours.
  • Mono: skip it. There's no tidy countdown, and the CDC's advice is simply not to kiss people who have it.
  • A cold sore: no lip contact from the first tingle until the sore has completely healed.

That's the cheat sheet. The reasons are more fun.

The Experiment Where Volunteers Kissed People With Colds

In 1984, a team of virus researchers published a study in The Journal of Infectious Diseases that reads like a dare. They gave volunteers a cold on purpose, using a rhinovirus (one of the classic cold viruses), then tried to pass it to healthy recipients who had no antibodies to that strain.

They ran three rounds:

  • Card night. Five infected volunteers and nine healthy ones spent two to three hours in a small room, playing cards and talking loudly. Nobody caught it.
  • The sleepover. Recipients lived in dormitory rooms for 36 hours, mostly in groups of two infected and two healthy people. One of 11 caught it.
  • The kiss. One of 13 recipients was infected after kissing an infected volunteer.

Across all three, 2 of 33 healthy volunteers got sick. The researchers' conclusion was blunt: rhinovirus colds are "difficult to transmit by short-term natural exposure."

Then comes my favorite detail. The same paper measured how much virus it took to infect people through different doors. Inside the nose, a tiny dose did the job. On the tongue, it took roughly 8,000 times more virus to infect the same share of people.

Your nose is a screen door. Your tongue is a bank vault.

Before you kiss everyone with a runny nose: these were small experiments, decades old, built around one type of virus, and the CDC notes that more than 200 respiratory viruses can cause colds. Treat the findings as a reason to relax, not a permission slip.

For Colds, Your Hands Do the Dirty Work

Six years earlier, researchers had tested the other route. In a 1978 study titled, with admirable directness, "Hand-to-hand transmission of rhinovirus colds," hand contact started an infection in 11 of 15 exposures. Large airborne droplets managed it once in 12 tries. Fine airborne particles went zero for 10.

The hands were simply carrying more cargo. The virus turned up in 65% of the infected volunteers' hand rinses and 39% of their saliva samples, and in 20 of 28 ten-second hand contacts it moved straight onto the healthy person's fingers. The researchers concluded that hand contact, followed by touching your own face, may be an important natural route for rhinovirus colds.

The CDC says the same thing in plainer English: some cold viruses spread through close contact "such as shaking hands with an infected person and then touching your eyes, nose, or mouth."

Which means the riskiest part of kissing someone with a cold might be everything around the kiss. You hold their hand. You cradle their face, right next to the nose they've been blowing all day. Then you rub your own eye.

It also makes the gallant hand kiss the worst possible sick-day gesture. That hand has been in and out of a tissue box since breakfast. Save the hand kiss for when they're well.

None of this makes lips a safe zone. The CDC says most respiratory viruses travel in droplets released when people cough or sneeze, and at kissing distance you're inside the splash zone whether your lips touch or not. The hygiene that matters happens around the kiss: wash your hands, keep your fingers off your own face and drink from your own mug.

Flu and COVID: Skip the Kiss Until They're Over the Hump

Flu isn't a cold with ambition.

According to the CDC, flu spreads mainly through droplets made when people cough, sneeze or talk, and those droplets "can land in the mouths or noses of people who are nearby." People are most contagious in the first three days of their illness, and the virus can be detected from one day before symptoms start until five to seven days after.

A kiss puts your mouth exactly where those droplets are aimed.

The CDC's current rule for respiratory viruses is the cleanest timeline I know. You can return to normal activities once, for at least 24 hours, your symptoms are getting better overall and you haven't had a fever without fever-reducing medication. For the next five days, the CDC recommends added precautions, including physical distancing, because you may still be able to spread the virus. For COVID-19, it notes that a test can help you judge how likely you are to pass it on.

Kissing is many things. Physical distancing is not one of them.

My rule of thumb: no lip kisses during the worst of it. After the 24-hour mark, a quick closed-mouth kiss is a call the two of you make together. The long, lazy make-out can wait out the five days.

Strep Throat: Let the Antibiotics Do Their Job

Strep throat is a bacterial infection, and it's a household favorite. The CDC says group A strep spreads through respiratory droplets or direct contact, including sharing a plate, utensils or a glass with someone who's infected. When one person in a household has strep throat, the bacteria often spread to the others.

The CDC's return-to-normal point is clear: no fever, plus antibiotics for at least 12 to 24 hours. Ask their healthcare provider how long they should stay home. Use the same line for kissing, and stop sharing the water glass on the nightstand while you're at it.

Mono: The Original Kissing Disease

Mono earned its nickname honestly. The Epstein-Barr virus that causes most cases spreads through bodily fluids, primarily saliva: kissing, shared drinks and food, shared cups, utensils and toothbrushes.

Three things make mono different from a cold:

  • There's no tidy clock. The CDC says that when you first catch the virus, you can spread it for weeks, and even before you have symptoms.
  • A peck counts. KidsHealth puts it plainly: "it is possible to get mono from just a quick peck on the lips." It isn't guaranteed, but it's possible.
  • It's slow. Typical mono symptoms show up four to six weeks after infection.

Here's the odd comfort: the CDC says about 9 out of 10 adults have antibodies showing a current or past Epstein-Barr infection, so there's a fair chance your body has met this virus before. That still doesn't make kissing someone in the middle of mono a good idea. The CDC's advice on mononucleosis is to avoid kissing people who have it, full stop.

This is the one where I'd stop negotiating. Ask their doctor when it's sensible to pick back up.

Cold Sores: From the First Tingle to Fully Healed

Despite the name, cold sores have nothing to do with colds. They're usually caused by herpes simplex virus type 1, and the NHS is unusually specific about the timing: cold sores are contagious "from the moment you first feel tingling" until the sore "has completely healed." Its advice is equally specific: do not kiss anyone while you have one.

Most cold sores clear up on their own within about 10 days, so this is a short sentence, not a life sentence.

Two more things worth knowing:

  • Babies are a hard no. The NHS warns that kissing a baby while you have a cold sore can lead to neonatal herpes, which is very dangerous to newborns.
  • It's not only about lips. The World Health Organization advises people with symptoms of oral herpes to avoid oral contact with others, and that covers more than kissing.

And let me be direct, because cold sores come with a side of shame they don't deserve. The WHO estimates that 3.8 billion people under 50, about 64%, carry HSV-1. Most adults have it. The risk of passing it on is greatest when sores are present, though it can spread without visible symptoms too. A cold sore isn't a character flaw. Kissing with one is just bad timing.

Not Every Kiss Carries the Same Risk

"Should we kiss?" is really a sliding scale. Here's how I'd rank the options, from least shared to most:

  • A blown kiss from the doorway. Zero contact. Ridiculous. Weirdly effective.
  • A kiss on the top of the head or the back of the shoulder. No saliva, and your face isn't directly in front of theirs.
  • A cheek or forehead kiss. No saliva swap, but you're face to face, breathing the same air.
  • A closed-mouth peck. A little saliva contact. Lower risk than a long kiss, and still enough to pass mono.
  • An open-mouth or French kiss. The full exchange. One study of 21 couples estimated that a ten-second intimate kiss moves around 80 million bacteria from one mouth to the other.

The drier and shorter the kiss, the less you share. Less isn't none.

And the idea that kissing gives your immune system a workout? It's unproven (more in what kissing does to your body), and it was never about kissing someone at the peak of the flu.

How to Turn Down a Kiss Without Bruising Anyone

Turning down a kiss can sting even when the reason is a fever. A few lines make it painless.

If you're the sick one, say it first. Don't wait until they're mid-lean and you have to swerve. Try: "I want to kiss you so badly, and I refuse to give you this. Rain check for Saturday?" You've turned a no into a promise.

If you're the healthy one, name the real target. "I'm not dodging you. I'm dodging the bug." Then pull their feet into your lap on the couch. Affection doesn't need lips to land.

If they decline your kiss, read it as care. Some people have more on the line than a lost weekend: a newborn at home, a wedding on Saturday, a long flight, or a doctor who's told them to steer clear of infections.

One sick partner is a bad week. Two is a plague o' both your houses, as Mercutio put it in Romeo and Juliet. Somebody has to stay well enough to make the soup.

The Sick-Day Kiss Menu

When lips are off the table, these still say "I'm here":

  • The crown kiss. A kiss on the top of the head while they're wrapped in a blanket is protective, tender and nowhere near their cough.
  • The forehead kiss. A forehead kiss works for a sniffle. For flu, you're still face to face, so keep it quick.
  • The shoulder kiss. Through a T-shirt, from behind, while they're slumped over their soup. A kiss on the shoulder says "I see you" without a single germ changing hands.
  • The blown kiss. From across the room, with full theatrical commitment. Bonus points for a dramatic catch on their end.
  • The tea delivery. Not technically a kiss. Counts double.

Whatever you choose, wash your hands afterward, before they wander anywhere near your own face.

Already Kissed Them? Here's What to Watch

Don't panic. A kiss isn't a guarantee. Remember the 12 of 13 volunteers who kissed someone with a cold and stayed well, and KidsHealth is clear that kissing someone with mono "doesn't mean you will get it."

The CDC's timelines tell you when to pay attention:

  • Flu: symptoms typically begin about two days after infection, with a range of one to four.
  • Strep throat: it usually takes two to five days after exposure to get sick.
  • Mono: typical symptoms appear four to six weeks after infection.

If something does show up, the same rules apply in reverse: you're now the one who skips the kiss. And if symptoms feel severe or aren't improving, call a healthcare provider rather than a search engine.

The First Kiss Back

The best part of being sick is the first real kiss after it. Make it worth the wait.

Brush, floss and drink some water first; a week of congestion does your breath no favors (fresh breath before kissing has the full routine). Tissues and dry winter air can leave lips raw, so if yours are flaking, kissing with chapped lips has the fixes.

Then take your time. Slow beats making up for lost time. And if the comeback lands in mistletoe season, you've got an excuse with a twig attached.

The Bottom Line

Can you kiss someone with a cold? Usually, yes: the kiss itself is a surprisingly weak way to pass one, and the hands and shared air around it do more of the work. Flu, COVID, strep, mono and cold sores are a different story, and each comes with its own clock.

Even the cautious official advice keeps things in proportion. Victoria's Better Health Channel tells you to "avoid kissing when you or the other person is sick," and in the same breath says "there is no need to give up kissing for the sake of your health." That's the whole philosophy. Skip the kiss for a few days. Never skip the affection.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you kiss someone with a cold?

Usually, yes, if you both accept a small risk. In a 1984 study, only 1 of 13 healthy volunteers caught a cold after kissing an infected volunteer. Hands and shared air spread colds more easily, so wash your hands and keep your fingers away from your own face.

Can you catch a cold from kissing?

You can, but it isn't the most likely route. In lab experiments, a cold virus spread far more efficiently through hand contact followed by touching the face. It also took roughly 8,000 times more virus to infect people through the tongue than through the nose.

How long after a cold can you kiss someone?

There's no official countdown for colds, which usually last less than a week. The CDC's general rule for respiratory viruses is 24 hours of improving symptoms with no fever before returning to normal activities, and that's a sensible bar for kissing too.

Can you kiss someone with the flu?

It's best not to. Flu spreads mainly through droplets from coughing, sneezing and talking, and people are most contagious in the first three days. Wait until they've had 24 hours of improving symptoms without a fever, then keep kisses brief for the next five days.

Can you kiss someone with strep throat?

Not until they reach the CDC's return-to-normal point: no fever and at least 12 to 24 hours of antibiotics. Strep spreads through respiratory droplets and shared cups or utensils, and it often passes between people who live together.

Can you get mono from a quick kiss?

Yes, it's possible. The virus that causes mono travels in saliva, so even a quick peck can pass it, though infection isn't guaranteed. The CDC advises against kissing people who have mono, and people can spread the virus for weeks, sometimes before symptoms appear.

Can you kiss someone with a cold sore?

No. The NHS says cold sores are contagious from the first tingle until they have completely healed, and advises not kissing anyone while you have one. Never kiss a baby when you have a cold sore: it can cause neonatal herpes, which is very dangerous for newborns.

Should you stop kissing a sick partner if you live together?

It still helps. The CDC advises people with respiratory symptoms to stay away from others, including people they live with who aren't sick. Skipping lip kisses, washing your hands and not sharing cups all lower your exposure, even under the same roof.
