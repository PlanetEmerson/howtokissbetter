When to Kiss on a Date: First Date, Second or Third?

Should you kiss on the first date? How to decide, the best moments during the date, exact words to say, and what it means when nobody goes for it.

The Short Answer

The right time to kiss on a date, including the first one, is a pause where you're close, the talk slows and neither of you moves to leave: usually the goodbye, a stop on the walk between places, or the moment after the check. You don't need to wait for a certain date number; nearly two in three singles say a kiss on the first date is fine. If you can't read the moment, ask, or save it for date two, because a first date without a kiss is not a bad sign.

I once spent most of a dinner date in Williamsburg calculating the exact second to kiss someone. Conversational lulls, wine consumed, ambient noise level: I had a whole internal spreadsheet going.

Then my date leaned across the table, put a hand on mine, and said, "Are you going to kiss me or just keep staring at my mouth?"

That's the trouble with timing a kiss from inside your own head. The answer is sitting across the table, and it's easier to read than it feels. This guide covers the three timing questions people actually ask: whether to kiss on the first date, how many dates to wait if not, and which moment during the date to use. Plus the exact words for when you can't tell.

Should You Kiss on the First Date?

Yes, if you both want to. There's no rule against it, and most people don't hold one.

When Match surveyed more than 5,000 U.S. singles for its 2017 Singles in America study, 64% said kissing is appropriate on a first date. Only 37% said the same about just hanging out. The word date does real work: once you've both agreed the evening is romantic, a kiss at the end is on the table for most people.

A first-date kiss also tells you something. Oxford researchers asked 902 adults whether their attraction to someone had ever changed after an initial kiss, and women, along with people who rated themselves as more attractive, were more likely to report it. The authors took that as evidence that kissing helps people size up a potential partner (Wlodarski and Dunbar, 2013). That cuts both ways. A kiss on date one gets you an answer sooner, and it's also why the moment feels so loaded.

Here's the three-question test I run before a first-date kiss:

  • Would I be disappointed if tonight ended with a hug? If not, skip the kiss. Kissing to be polite sends a message you don't mean.
  • Have they closed the distance at least once? A hand on your arm, a chair pulled closer, a walk that keeps drifting into your shoulder. One move from them, not only from you.
  • Is a close, fairly private moment coming? A walk, a car, a doorway. If the night ends in a loud bar next to their friends, the kiss can wait.

Three yeses: go, or ask. Two: ask. One or none: end on a warm goodbye and plan the second date. Holding off when the signs are thin is just reading the room, and it makes date two easier to get.

How Many Dates Before a First Kiss?

No number works for everyone, but the situation you're in narrows it down a lot.

You met on an app and this is the first time in person

The first meeting is partly a fact check: does this person match the profile, and do you like being around them? A kiss can happen, but it shouldn't be the goal. If the date runs long, moves to a second spot, or ends with both of you dragging your feet, that's your opening. If it was a pleasant 45-minute coffee, a hug and a clear "I'd like to see you again" does more for date two than a rushed kiss.

You already know each other

Friends, coworkers, the person you've been circling at parties for months. You've done the getting-to-know-you part, and the tension has had time to build. A kiss on the first official date isn't early here; it might be overdue. (If the friendship is the thing you're scared to lose, kissing a friend has its own rules.)

You've been texting for weeks first

Weeks of messages build a closeness the first meeting has to catch up to. Plenty of people are bold on the phone and shy in person. Give the in-person version an hour or two to match the texting version before you move.

You're dating again after a long gap

Divorced, widowed, fresh out of a long relationship, dating in your 40s or 50s: the pace is yours to set. Nobody is keeping score. If you'd rather slow things down, say so warmly and early, and it reads as self-assured rather than cold.

My rule of thumb: if the first two dates were good and nobody has kissed, the third date is where you say something out loud. Two good dates without a kiss often means two careful people waiting for each other to go first.

The Best Moments to Kiss on a Date

The best date kisses happen in a pause the date hands you. You don't have to build one from scratch. You have to notice it, and not fill it with small talk about parking.

1. The stop on the walk between places

Dinner to drinks, the bar to the train, a wander because neither of you wants the night to end. Somebody stops walking to finish a story, you both turn, and the street keeps moving around you. That stop is my favorite kiss window of any date, because it's private without being a big production.

2. Right after the check

The plates are gone, the bill is paid, and nobody has stood up yet. The official part of the date is over and the "what now?" hangs in the air. If you're sitting side by side, this can be the moment. If you're across a table, stand up first. A kiss over the bread basket is a contact sport.

3. After a real laugh or a real confession

Two different moods, same opening. After a big shared laugh you're both loose and a little breathless. After your date tells you something they don't tell everyone, there's a quiet that asks for a response. With the second one, let the words land first. Hold eye contact for a beat, then move. Go too fast and it looks like you weren't listening.

4. The car or the wait for the ride home

Walking them to their car, sitting in yours before they get out, standing on the curb while their ride is three minutes away. The date is technically over and neither of you is moving to end it. Don't fill the silence by hunting for your keys. (Kissing across a center console is its own puzzle, so here's how to kiss in a car without the gearshift winning.)

5. The doorstep

The classic, and the one with the most built-in pressure, because you both know it's coming. It works because it has a natural ending: kiss, smile, goodnight. Keep it short, or it turns into an unplanned "do you want to come in?" negotiation. I've broken down the goodnight kiss step by step, from the pause to the pull-back.

And the moments that almost never work: the hello (you have no information yet), across a table in the middle of the meal, in front of their friends, while the server is standing right there, and in the back seat of a rideshare with a driver watching the mirror.

Should You Kiss Mid-Date or at the End?

Most people seem to save it for the end. It's what the movies taught us, and it feels safe: by the goodbye, you've read the whole evening.

The catch is that saving it turns the date into an audition. You laugh at a joke, and some part of you is scoring whether the laugh improved your odds. A mid-date kiss ends the audition. You both know where you stand, and the rest of the night gets to be fun instead of a countdown.

My rule: if the pull shows up by the midpoint (you're touching easily, the talk has turned personal, you've caught them looking at your mouth), don't bank it for the doorstep. Use the next walk or the next pause. If you're still unsure at the midpoint, wait. The goodbye gives you the whole night's evidence, and a slow build has its own charge.

How Do You Know It's the Right Moment?

For timing, I look for three things landing at once:

  • Close: you're within arm's reach, and they drifted in or stayed put when you did.
  • Quiet: the talk slows, sentences trail off, and the pauses feel full instead of awkward.
  • Staying: they could leave, get in the car, or open the door, and they don't.

Any one of those alone means little. All three together, after an evening of easy touch, is about as clear an invitation as you'll get without words. For the full list of tells, from the eyes-to-lips glance to the signs that mean "not yet," see how to tell if someone wants to kiss you.

How to Go for the Kiss (or Ask for It)

You've found the moment. The gap between knowing and doing now feels about a mile wide. Three moves close it.

Stop talking. Let the sentence end, hold eye contact for two seconds, and don't fill the silence. It will feel like twenty. That silence is what makes the kiss feel like a shared choice instead of an ambush.

Go 90% of the way. Lean in slowly enough that they can meet you or ease back, then pause just short of their lips. The last 10% is theirs. If they close it, that's your yes. If they pull back, smile and let it go.

Keep it short. A first kiss on a date only needs a few seconds. Pull back, smile, and let it breathe. If they lean back in, you'll know.

If you can't read them, ask. Asking doesn't kill the mood when you say it low, with eye contact and a half-smile. Lines that fit each moment:

  • At the door or the car: "I'd really like to kiss you."
  • On the walk, when you've both stopped: "Can I kiss you?"
  • When the signals are mixed: "I've been wanting to kiss you all night. Is that okay?"
  • When you'd rather wait but want them to know: "I'm going to kiss you on our second date. Fair warning."

More phrasings, and how to handle a no gracefully, are in how to ask for a kiss. The lean-in itself gets the full treatment in how to initiate a kiss.

When Not to Kiss on a Date

Some dates don't have a kiss in them, and forcing one does more damage than skipping it.

  • They pulled back. You reached for their hand and they moved it, or you moved closer and they shifted to keep the gap. That's an answer. Respect it completely.
  • They've checked out. Short replies, phone out, eyes on the room. A kiss won't rescue the evening.
  • Either of you has had too much to drink. A little loosening up is fine. If anyone is slurring or swaying, it's a goodnight hug and a text tomorrow.
  • You're not sure you want a second date. A kiss reads as "I'm interested." Don't hand it out to be polite.
  • They told you they like to take things slow. Believe them, and say you're happy to wait. That lands better than any kiss would.

Ending without a kiss doesn't have to be awkward. Step in for a hug, hold it a second longer than you'd hug a friend, and say: "I had a really good time. I'd like to do this again." That's a complete ending.

If you're the one who wants to wait and you can feel a kiss coming, you can slow it down without cooling things off. Turn it into a hug or a kiss on the cheek, and say something like: "I'm a slow starter, but I definitely want a second date." It's clear, and it gives them something to look forward to.

What If You Missed the Moment (or Nobody Kissed)?

You saw the opening and froze. Now you're replaying it on the way home. Relax: moments come back. If the connection is real, the night or the next date will open another one, and first-kiss nerves are normal, not a verdict on you.

Three ways to recover:

  • Make a new pause before the night ends. The walk to the car, the goodbye, the "one more thing" as they turn to go.
  • Name it. "I should have kissed you back there." It's honest, a little vulnerable, and it often opens a second chance on the spot.
  • Text it. "Had a great time tonight. Also kicking myself for not kissing you when you were telling me about your sister's wedding. Next time." It tells them you wanted to, and it sets up date two.

And if nobody kissed on the first date? That's not a bad sign. People skip a first-date kiss because they're nervous, because they like to wait, or because the goodbye happened somewhere crowded. Whether they say yes to a second date, and how the texts feel afterward, tells you far more. If date two is good and still kiss-free, use the third-date rule: say something out loud.

The right time was never a date number. It's the pause where you're close, it's quiet, and neither of you is leaving. Catch that one, and then what you do after the kiss matters more than the second you picked.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I kiss on the first date?

You can, if you both clearly want to. In Match's 2017 Singles in America survey, 64% of singles said kissing is appropriate on a first date. Look for a moment when you're close, the conversation slows and neither of you is moving to leave. If you can't tell, ask, or save it for the second date.

How many dates before a first kiss?

There's no set number; it depends on how well you already know each other. Someone you've known for months might kiss you on the first date, while an app match you're meeting for the first time might take two or three. If you've had two good dates and nobody has kissed, use the third to say it out loud or ask.

Is it normal to kiss on the first date?

Yes. Most singles think a first-date kiss is fine: 64% said so in Match's survey of more than 5,000 U.S. singles. It's just as normal not to. Both are fine as long as you both want what's happening.

Is it bad if you don't kiss on the first date?

No. People skip a first-date kiss because they're nervous, because they like to wait, or because the goodbye happened somewhere crowded. Whether they agree to a second date, and how they text afterward, tells you far more about their interest than a missing kiss.

Is it better to kiss mid-date or at the end?

Both work. A mid-date kiss, on a walk between places or right after the check, removes the "will we or won't we" pressure and lets you enjoy the rest of the night. An end-of-date kiss is the safer choice when you're unsure, because by then you've read the whole evening.

How do I know if my date wants to be kissed?

Look for three things at once: they're close to you, the conversation has slowed into comfortable pauses, and they're lingering when they could leave. Their eyes dropping to your lips and touch that has grown more frequent through the evening make the signal stronger. One sign alone means little; together they're a clear invitation.

What if I miss the moment to kiss on a date?

Moments come back. Make a new pause before the night ends, like the walk to the car or the goodbye, or just say it: "I should have kissed you back there." If the night is over, a text that says you wish you'd kissed them sets up the next date nicely.
