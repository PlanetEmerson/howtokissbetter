Never Been Kissed at 25 or 30? Here's How to Get Your First Kiss

Never been kissed in your 20s or 30s? What's really in the way, how adults get a first kiss, whether to tell them it's your first, and what they'll notice.

The Short Answer

No, it isn't weird to have never been kissed at 25 or 30. It usually comes down to opportunity and nerves, not anything wrong with you, and it says nothing about how you'll kiss. To change it, go on more dates with room for a quiet moment, ask for the kiss out loud, and tell them it's your first only if saying so calms you down.

You're 25, or 30, or old enough that you've stopped believing anyone would believe you, and you've never kissed anyone. Maybe nobody knows. Maybe you've gotten very good at going quiet when friends start trading first-kiss stories.

Here's what I want you to hear first: this is one of the most fixable situations in dating. It isn't a flaw in you, and it doesn't decide what kind of kisser you'll be. It's a gap, usually in opportunity, nerve or both, and gaps close on purpose.

The numbers on who kisses when live in my guide to the average age of a first kiss. This one is about what to do next: whether it's weird, what's in the way, how adults actually get a first kiss, and whether to tell the person it's your first.

Is It Weird to Never Have Been Kissed at 25?

No. It feels weird because almost nobody says it out loud, so everyone in your position assumes they're the only one.

You aren't. In a 2018 survey of 738 first-year students at a US university, 14.2%, about one in seven, had never kissed a partner on the lips. Those were college freshmen, not 25- or 30-year-olds, so it doesn't tell you how many people are still waiting at your age. It does show that plenty of people reach adulthood without a first kiss.

Nothing about turning 18 or 25 makes a kiss happen. You need a person you like, a private-enough moment, and one of you willing to move. If you spent your teens shy, busy, closeted, in a strict home, or simply not interested yet, those three rarely showed up together. That's a story about circumstances. It isn't a verdict.

Try a better test than "is it weird?" Picture someone you're dating telling you, a little nervously, that you'd be their first kiss. Do you think less of them? Or do you feel a bit trusted, maybe a bit flattered? The judgment you're bracing for lives mostly in your own head.

What does cost you is the hiding. Turning down dates so nobody finds out. Steering every conversation away from exes. Deciding you're "not a relationship person" before you've tried. That's what keeps a first kiss away. The inexperience itself is a few seconds from being over.

What's Actually Stopping You?

Be honest about which of these fits, because each one has a different fix.

  • Nobody to kiss. You can't remember your last date, or there hasn't been one. Often that's the whole story, and it's the easiest one to change, because it's logistics. Start with the next section.
  • Somebody, but you froze. There was a walk home or a long goodbye where they were clearly waiting, and you let it pass. Opportunity isn't your problem. The last two feet are. Read whether to tell them and what they'll notice, then my guide to first-kiss nerves.
  • You're not sure you want it. Some people feel pressure to be kissed long before they feel any pull toward kissing someone. If the idea leaves you cold rather than scared, listen to that instead of overriding it. It can be a plain preference, or part of being asexual or aromantic, and why some people don't like kissing walks through it. Nobody is required to reach this milestone at all.

How to Get Your First Kiss When You're Older

Teenagers stumble into first kisses at parties and on dares. Adults mostly kiss on dates. The plan, then, is less about kissing and more about putting yourself in enough of the right evenings.

Get on the calendar. Pick one or two routes and give them a few months: a dating app with recent photos and a profile that says what you actually like doing, friends you've told plainly that you'd like to be set up, a weekly class or club where you see the same faces. One date a season won't do it. A steady run of first dates will, partly because plenty of first dates go nowhere. That's true for everyone, not just you.

Plan dates with a second act. Coffee with a hard stop gives a kiss nowhere to happen. Dinner and then a walk, or a drink and then dessert somewhere else, creates the pauses where kisses live: the stop between places, the wait for a ride, the doorstep. My guide on when to kiss on a date ranks those moments.

Say yes to the second and third date. If the first one was pleasant and you're curious, go again. Comfort builds, and touch builds with it: a hand on an arm, sitting closer, a hug that lasts a beat longer. By the time the kiss comes, it should feel like the next small step, not a leap off a cliff.

Ask, out loud. You've waited this long; you don't also have to gamble on a perfectly timed lean-in. "Can I kiss you?" said quietly, with eye contact, is clear, kind and hard to get wrong. It also hands them the easiest possible yes. Here's how to ask for a kiss without it sounding stiff.

One idea to drop: that your first kiss has to be with The One. It needs to be with someone you like, trust and actually want to kiss. That bar is high enough.

Should You Tell Them You've Never Been Kissed?

You don't owe anyone your history. Tell them if saying it would calm you down. Keep it to yourself if saying it would make the moment heavier. Both are honest.

If you do tell them, three things help:

  • Before the moment, not during it. Mid-lean, the line lands like an emergency brake. On the walk, over a drink, or once things are clearly heading somewhere, it lands like trust.
  • Lightly, and once. Your tone sets theirs. Say it like a confession and they'll treat it as a big deal. Say it with a half-smile and they'll follow you there.
  • Without the backstory. They don't need the full history of why. One sentence covers it.

Lines you can borrow:

  • "Fair warning: if tonight goes where I think it's going, you'd be my first kiss."
  • "I've never actually kissed anyone. I'd like to change that, and I'd like it to be with you."
  • "Late bloomer. Go easy on me." (Smile when you say it.)

Expect surprise, and then something warm. Some people will want to make it special, which is lovely. If someone mocks you for it, that tells you something about them, not about you, and you found out before you kissed them instead of after.

If you'd rather keep it private, that's fine too. A first kiss with anyone new is a little clumsy, whatever their history, so your inexperience has plenty of cover in the moment.

Will They Be Able to Tell It's Your First Kiss?

Less than you fear. Psychologists have a name for the feeling that everything inside you is on display: the illusion of transparency. In a set of experiments published in 1998, Thomas Gilovich and colleagues found that people consistently overestimate how much others can read their inner states. Liars thought their lies were easier to spot than they were, and people thought their disgust was more obvious than it really was. A 2003 follow-up found the same with nerves: people giving a speech believed their nervousness was more obvious to the audience than it was. Your nerves are probably much louder inside your head than on your face.

What a partner picks up on isn't inexperience. It's what nerves do to a kiss: lunging in, stiff lips, holding your breath, pulling away too fast. A few habits cover almost all of it:

  • Go slower than feels natural. Lean most of the way in and let them close the last inch.
  • Keep the first one short and closed-mouth. A few seconds of relaxed lips, then ease back and smile. Tongue can wait for a later kiss.
  • Breathe through your nose. It sounds too obvious to mention, right up until you're holding your breath.
  • Follow their lead. Match their pressure and pace. Paying attention reads as skill, and it's the part experience can't replace.

Whether you're any good won't come from your history. It shows up in how they respond, and those cues are in the signs you're a good kisser.

Never Been Kissed at 30 or Older

Everything above still applies. A few things shift.

The people you date now usually have some history of their own, and that tends to help. A grown-up date is weighing whether you're kind, present and into them. Your kissing résumé isn't on the list.

Keeping it quiet can also feel heavier with time, and it may come bundled with other firsts. If several of yours are likely to arrive in the same relationship, say so early, privately, and before things get physical: not on a first date, but once you both know you like each other. Something like: "I'm less experienced than you'd probably guess. I'd like to take it slow, and I'd like it to be with you." A good partner hears that as trust and gives you the patience you asked for.

And if what has kept you single is a fear of being judged or embarrassed, strong enough to keep you away from social situations, take that seriously on its own terms. That's the territory of social anxiety, and it's treatable: the NHS lists cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) as a treatment, along with guided self-help built on it. Getting help with that isn't a detour from your first kiss. It's often the shortest road there.

The day after your first kiss, "never been kissed" stops being a fact about you. The years before it shrink into a detail you can mention or not, whenever you like.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it normal to never have been kissed at 30?

Yes. It's more common than it feels, because people rarely say it out loud. A first kiss depends on opportunity, nerves and timing, not on your age or your worth, and kissing is learned by doing at any age.

Should I tell my date I've never been kissed?

Only if it helps you. Saying it can take the pressure off: tell them before the moment, lightly, in one sentence, like "Fair warning, you'd be my first kiss." You don't owe anyone your history, and keeping it private is just as honest.

Can someone tell if it's your first kiss?

Not as easily as you fear. People tend to overestimate how much of what they feel shows on the outside, a bias psychologists call the illusion of transparency. What a partner notices is rushing, stiffness or holding your breath, so go slowly, keep the first kiss short and closed-mouth, and follow their pace.

How do you get your first kiss as an adult?

Go on more dates, and plan them with a walk or a second stop so a quiet moment can happen. Let touch build over a second or third date. When the moment comes and you can't read them, ask: "Can I kiss you?" Clear and kind beats a perfect lean-in.

Does your first kiss have to be with someone you're dating?

No. It only has to be with someone you like, trust and want to kiss. Waiting for a relationship is a fine choice too, as long as it's a choice and not a way to avoid trying.

What if I don't want to kiss anyone?

Then you don't have to. Not wanting to kiss can be a simple preference or part of being asexual or aromantic, and there's nothing to fix. Feeling pressure to reach a milestone isn't the same as wanting it.
