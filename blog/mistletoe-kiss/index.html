Mistletoe Kiss: The Forgotten Rules and How to Do It Right

Where kissing under the mistletoe came from, the one-berry-per-kiss rule nobody follows anymore and how to give, read or dodge a mistletoe kiss gracefully.

The Short Answer

A mistletoe kiss is the Christmas custom of kissing someone you end up standing under a sprig of mistletoe with. It first appears in English records in 1784, and the original rule was one kiss per berry: pluck a berry each time, and when the berries ran out, so did the kissing. Today it's an invitation, not an obligation. Point up, pause and let the other person choose; default to the cheek with anyone who isn't your partner, and treat a hug or a laugh as a complete answer.

Somebody at the party has hung mistletoe in the doorway between the kitchen and the living room. You know this because you've now walked under it four times, alone, carrying a plate of cheese like someone with an urgent appointment.

Then it happens. You and the person you've been half-watching all night reach the doorway from opposite sides at the same moment. Somebody across the room spots it. Somebody says "Ooooh." And a sprig of plant that weighs less than a teaspoon of sugar is suddenly running the room.

Here's what nobody tells you about the mistletoe kiss: the original version came with rules. Real ones, with a built-in limit, and they were smarter than anything we do with it now. Let me be direct about the modern version too. A mistletoe kiss is an invitation, never an obligation. The plant can set up the moment. Only the other person can say yes to it.

Below: where kissing under the mistletoe actually came from (the documented version, not the party trivia), the forgotten one-berry rule, how to give a mistletoe kiss that lands well, what it means depending on who steered you there and how to slip out of one without making anyone feel small.

What Is a Mistletoe Kiss?

A mistletoe kiss is a kiss shared by two people who end up standing beneath a hanging sprig of mistletoe during the Christmas season. By custom, anyone caught under it is "owed" a kiss, usually a quick one on the cheek or the lips. The tradition first shows up in English records in the 1780s and spread through 19th-century books and Christmas parties.

In practice, it's a playful excuse. Mistletoe answers the two awkward logistical questions in any kiss, where and when, and leaves the most important one to you: do they want to?

The quick read on what one means:

  • From your partner: A ritual and a bit of theater. "I'm still choosing you, even in a doorway."
  • From a crush who "happened" to stop under it: Rarely an accident. The mistletoe is their alibi.
  • From a friend or relative, on the cheek: Holiday affection. Nothing more to decode.
  • From a coworker at the office party: Usually a misjudgment. More on that below.
  • From someone who has parked under it all night: They're running a fishing operation. Admire the patience; you're not obligated to bite.

Why Do We Kiss Under the Mistletoe? The Honest History

Look up the origin story and you'll get confident tales of Druids, Norse gods and ancient fertility rites. Some of that is real. Most of it has nothing to do with kissing.

The plant was sacred long before it was flirty. Mistletoe grows on trees and stays green after its host has dropped its leaves, which made it look downright magical in midwinter. In the first century, Pliny the Elder wrote that druids in Gaul cut mistletoe from oak trees with a golden sickle and believed it could cure barrenness in animals and work as an antidote to poison. There's no kissing anywhere in his account.

The Norse myth doesn't end the way the internet says. In the story, the god Baldur is killed by a dart made of mistletoe, the one plant his mother Frigg never asked to swear it would spare him. The popular version has Frigg forgiving the plant and promising a kiss to anyone who passes beneath it. That happy ending is a later retelling. In the Prose Edda, the 13th-century Icelandic source, Baldur stays in the underworld.

The kissing is surprisingly recent. English Heritage points to the comic opera Two to One, written in 1784, as the earliest known record of kissing beneath the mistletoe. By the early 1800s it was an established Christmas custom in English farmhouses and kitchens.

Then two famous writers made it charming. Washington Irving described the custom in the Christmas sketches of his Sketch Book in 1820, with the mistletoe hung up "to the imminent peril of all the pretty housemaids." (In 1820 that was a joke. Read it now and it lands more like a warning.) His book helped popularize the custom with American readers.

In Dickens's The Pickwick Papers, published in 1836 and 1837, old Mr. Pickwick takes an elderly lady by the hand, leads her beneath "the mystic branch" and kisses her "in all courtesy and decorum," while the younger guests dissolve into what Dickens calls "general and most delightful struggling and confusion."

Read that chapter today and it's half charming, half HR complaint. Which brings us to the rule everyone forgot.

The Forgotten Rule: One Berry, One Kiss

Irving's footnote spells it out. The young men had "the privilege of kissing the girls under it, plucking each time a berry from the bush. When the berries are all plucked the privilege ceases." English Heritage describes the same tradition with one extra detail: the gentleman plucked a berry and kissed the lady on the cheek.

Look at how well that was designed. Every kiss cost something. Everyone could see how many were left. And the game had a natural ending that required no awkward conversation: no berries, no more kisses.

We kept the plant and threw away the rules, which is a bit like keeping the referee's whistle and firing the referee.

Here's how I'd bring the berry rule back for grown-ups:

  • Every kiss is its own question. A yes under the mistletoe at 9 p.m. doesn't carry over to 11 p.m.
  • Either person can stop picking berries. The moment someone steps out, laughs it off or offers a cheek, the privilege ceases. Irving said so.
  • The cheek is the original setting. Lips are an upgrade both people choose, not the default.

Do You Have to Kiss Under the Mistletoe?

No. Not once, not ever.

Folklore disagrees, mostly to keep the game going. Debrett's, the British etiquette authority, notes that refusing a kiss under the mistletoe is traditionally considered bad luck, and one strand of old folklore claimed a woman who refused wouldn't get a marriage proposal all year. Superstitions like that have been doing a lot of unpaid work for a few centuries.

The same Debrett's guide gets the modern part right: pick a willing partner, always ask first and no grabbing. A plant hanging from a doorframe has never once been a substitute for a yes.

If anything, mistletoe makes asking easier. You don't have to invent a reason to bring up kissing. The reason is hanging right there, and pointing at it is about the lightest question you'll ever ask. How to Kiss Someone Under the Mistletoe

Here's the move, step by step. It works for a partner, a date or the crush you've been circling since the coats went on the bed.

  1. Notice who's lingering. Someone who slows down in the doorway, glances up and then glances at you is sending a signal. Someone who walks through at full speed while talking about traffic is not.
  2. Point up and smile. That tiny gesture is the whole question. It says "Look where we are" and hands them the answer.
  3. Read the answer in their face. A laugh, eye contact and staying put means yes. A laugh and a step back means no, said kindly.
  4. Lean most of the way and pause. Leave the last couple of inches for them. If they close the gap, you're in. If they offer a cheek, kiss the cheek and be glad about it.
  5. Keep your hands polite. Light fingertips on the upper arm or shoulder are plenty. This is a doorway at a party, not the final scene of a romcom.
  6. Keep it short. Two or three seconds reads as charming. Ten seconds in a busy doorway makes you the couple everyone is waiting to get past on the way to the bathroom.
  7. Step out from under it. Take the moment with you. Hovering under the mistletoe for round two turns a sweet kiss into a toll booth.

A few practical notes from the doorway:

  • Doorways love a height difference. You're both standing straight up, sometimes with one person on a step or threshold. If one of you is much taller, the height difference guide has the step and lean fixes that make it graceful.
  • Holiday lipstick travels. A deep berry shade on someone's cheek is festive for about four seconds. The lipstick kissing guide covers the blotting that keeps it on your mouth.
  • The cheese board is not your friend. Garlic dip and mulled wine linger. A mint before you wander anywhere near a doorway is cheap insurance.

Cheek or Lips? What a Mistletoe Kiss Means

Where the kiss lands tells you more than the plant ever will.

A kiss on the cheek

This is the traditional mistletoe kiss and the right default with friends, family, new acquaintances and anyone you're unsure about. Debrett's even notes it's customary to start with the other person's right cheek. A cheek kiss under the mistletoe is warm, festive and almost impossible to misread. For the finer shades, here's what a kiss on the cheek means in different situations.

A peck on the lips

Between partners, a peck under the mistletoe is the classic. Between two people who aren't together, it means more, because someone chose lips when the cheek was right there. That's usually interest wearing a holiday costume. A peck on the lips from someone who pecks everyone means less than one from someone who never does.

Anything longer

Save it for later and somewhere quieter. A full make-out in the doorway turns everyone else in the room into an audience that never bought a ticket. The same courtesy that applies to kissing in public applies to a crowded living room.

When a crush keeps "accidentally" ending up under it

That's not an accident. Mistletoe is the world's most plausible excuse, which is why people who've wanted to kiss you for weeks suddenly develop a deep interest in doorways. Mariah Carey has been waiting under the mistletoe on every holiday playlist since 1994, and she was never waiting for just anyone.

Pair the doorway with the other tells, like eyes drifting to your mouth and no hurry to leave, and the signs someone wants to kiss you start lining up. Then let it be mutual: point up, pause and let them choose.

The Office Party Rule

Mistletoe at the office party should be decorative.

Debrett's puts it with very British understatement: "grasping the boss in a drunken embrace will be an embarrassing memory the morning after." I'd go further. Anyone who controls your schedule, your raise or your reviews is off the mistletoe list, and so are you for anyone who reports to you. A power gap turns "it's just tradition" into pressure, even when nobody means it that way.

If you end up under it with a coworker, laugh, point up, say "Not this year, mistletoe" and keep walking. Everyone remembers the person who handled it gracefully. Nobody has to talk to HR in January.

How to Get Out of a Mistletoe Kiss Without Making It Weird

Sometimes you land in the doorway with someone you don't want to kiss. Your uncle. Your ex. The coworker who replies-all. The plant has no idea, and you owe it nothing.

The trick is to decline the kiss without declining the person. A few lines that work:

  • "The berries are all gone." Bonus points for knowing your history. Nobody argues with Washington Irving.
  • "Hug instead?" Open your arms as you say it. A hug answers the moment and gives everyone an easy exit.
  • A high five. Ridiculous, cheerful and impossible to take personally.
  • Keep walking. A smile and "Nice try, mistletoe" as you pass through is a complete sentence.

Brit + Co's etiquette guide suggests the same menu: an air kiss, a hug, a handshake or a high five instead.

And if you're the one who got the cheek, the hug or the high five? Smile, say "Fair," and move on. A graceful no is the other person being kind to you. Chasing it, joking about it twice or pointing at the mistletoe again is how a cute moment becomes the story they tell about you next December.

How to Make Mistletoe Work for a Couple

For couples, mistletoe is the rare holiday decoration that actually does something.

  • Hang it where you really cross paths. The kitchen doorway beats the formal front hall, because you'll pass under it together a dozen times a day.
  • Play the berry rule for real. Count the berries on day one, make every kiss under it cost one and find out who rations and who splurges. Faux berries work fine for this, and they're safer (more on that below).
  • Make it a pause, not a peck. The point of a ritual is that you stop for it. Take an extra beat, look at each other first and let the rest of the day wait three seconds. If it's been a while since you kissed like you meant it, why couples stop kissing is worth a read before December gets busy.

Hanging Mistletoe? Where to Put It (and the Safety Part)

If you're hosting, placement is etiquette.

  • Make it opt-in. Hang it in a doorway people can choose to pause in, not the only route to the bathroom or the front door. Nobody should have to run a gauntlet to find their coat.
  • Hang it high and secure. A sprig dangling at forehead height turns the party into a game of dodge.
  • Mind the berries and leaves. Real mistletoe is toxic if eaten. The ASPCA lists American mistletoe as toxic to dogs, cats and horses, and fallen berries are easy for toddlers and pets to find before you do. In homes with small kids or animals, a faux sprig is the smart call. If someone does swallow some, call Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222 in the US, or your vet for a pet.

Then step back and let the decoration do its job: start a conversation and, only if both people want it, a kiss.

The Bottom Line

The mistletoe kiss is a custom with about 240 years of practice behind it, and the original came with smart rules: one berry per kiss, the cheek as the default and a natural end when the berries ran out.

Bring those back and the tradition gets better, not stuffier. Point up, pause, let them choose and keep it short. If they offer a hug instead, take the hug.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why do people kiss under the mistletoe?

The custom appears in England in the late 1700s, and English Heritage cites a 1784 comic opera as the earliest known record. Mistletoe already had older associations with fertility and luck, and writers such as Washington Irving and Charles Dickens helped turn the kiss into a well-loved Christmas tradition in the 1800s. Nobody knows exactly who started it.

What is the mistletoe berry rule?

The original rule was one berry per kiss. Each time someone claimed a kiss under the mistletoe, they plucked a berry from the sprig, and when the berries were gone, the kissing privilege ended. Washington Irving recorded the rule in 1820.

Do you have to kiss someone under the mistletoe?

No. Folklore calls it bad luck to refuse, but a mistletoe kiss is an invitation, not an obligation. Ask or pause before leaning in, and accept a hug, a laugh or a step away as a complete answer.

Is a mistletoe kiss on the cheek or the lips?

Traditionally, the cheek. Default to the cheek with friends, family, coworkers and anyone you're unsure about. Partners, or two people who clearly both want it, can share a short kiss on the lips.

What does it mean if someone stands under the mistletoe with you?

If they lingered, looked up and smiled at you, it's usually a deliberate invitation and a sign of interest. If they were just passing through, it's a coincidence. How they react when you playfully point at the mistletoe will tell you which.

Where should you hang mistletoe?

In a doorway or archway that people can choose to pause in, high enough to clear everyone's heads, and not on the only route to the bathroom or the exit. Keep real mistletoe out of reach of children and pets, or use a faux sprig.

Is mistletoe poisonous?

Yes. Real mistletoe berries and leaves are toxic if eaten, and the ASPCA lists American mistletoe as toxic to dogs, cats and horses. Hang it out of reach, sweep up fallen berries and call Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222 in the US, or a vet for a pet, if someone swallows it.

How long should a mistletoe kiss last?

A few seconds. Two or three is the sweet spot in a busy doorway: long enough to feel like a real kiss, short enough that nobody behind you is waiting. Save anything longer for somewhere quieter.
