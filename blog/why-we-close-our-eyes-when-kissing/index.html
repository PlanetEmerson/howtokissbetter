Why Do We Close Our Eyes When We Kiss?

Probably so your brain can spend more of its attention on touch. What the one real study found, what it doesn't prove, and whether you're supposed to close them.

The Short Answer

We most likely close our eyes when we kiss so the brain can spend more of its attention on touch: in a 2016 Royal Holloway study, a harder visual task made people noticeably worse at telling whether a brief vibration had touched their hand, and one of the researchers suggested that shutting your eyes runs the same trade in reverse. Two smaller reasons help: nobody can focus on a face an inch away, and closed eyes feel like letting your guard down. It's a reflex, not a rule, so open eyes aren't a mistake.

Someone leans in. The gap between your faces shrinks to a few inches, then one, and somewhere in that last inch your eyelids drop.

You didn't decide that. Nobody taught you. For most people it just happens, the same way you tilt your head without being told.

Ask the internet why and you mostly get vibes: it's romantic, it's trust, it's your pupils. There is one real experiment behind the answer. It wasn't about kissing at all, and what it found is more useful than any of the vibes. Here's what it tested, what it proves, what it doesn't, and how to put it to work the next time your eyes close.

What the Royal Holloway Study Actually Found

In 2016, psychologists Sandra Murphy and Polly Dalton at Royal Holloway, University of London, published a short paper with a wonderful title: Out of Touch? Visual Load Induces Inattentional Numbness.

The setup was simple and a little absurd. Sixteen volunteers sat with their hands palm-up under a black cloth, white noise in their headphones, answering with foot pedals. A ring of six letters flashed on a screen for a tenth of a second, and they had to say which one it held: an X or an N. In the easy version, the target sat among plain o's and jumped out. In the hard version, it hid among look-alikes (H, K, M, V, W, Z) and took real focus. On half the trials, a vibration buzzed one palm for a fiftieth of a second, and afterward they said whether they had felt it.

With the easy search, they judged correctly whether there had been a buzz 89% of the time. With the hard search, that fell to 79%. Same buzz. Same hands. The only thing that changed was how hard their eyes were working.

The part I love: people didn't just get more cautious or more trigger-happy about saying yes. Their response bias stayed flat while their actual sensitivity dropped. Their sense of touch genuinely got fuzzier. The researchers named it inattentional numbness.

Dalton made the jump to kissing herself. Talking to reporters, she said the results imply that reducing what your eyes have to do, for example by shutting them, can sharpen your sense of touch, and that this could be one of the reasons that people shut their eyes when kissing.

What the study doesn't prove

Nobody in that lab kissed anyone. The study never tested lips, never tested closed eyes, and ran one experiment on sixteen people; the authors wrote that their claims hinge on that single experiment. What it does show is the direction of the trade: the more your eyes are doing, the less clearly you feel. Closing your eyes is the far end of that scale, zero visual work, so it's a fair bet that it pushes touch the other way.

The trade isn't a one-off, either. In a set of experiments by researchers at Oxford and University College London, up to 79% of people doing a hard visual task failed to notice a brief tone, far more than with an easy task. Busy eyes dull your ears as well as your skin.

Why Does a Kiss Feel Better With Your Eyes Closed?

Think of attention as a budget. Seeing, hearing and feeling partly draw from the same account, and the studies above show that busy eyes can drain it. Even at kissing range, where their face is a blur, your eyes keep sending in light, movement and color, and your brain keeps paying to process it.

Close your eyes and that spending stops, which likely leaves more for what's left: the give of their lower lip, the warmth of their breath across your cheek, the exact second their hand slides to your jaw. Your lips are wired for that kind of detail, and with the lights off they get more of the budget.

People do this whenever they need to focus. You close your eyes to catch the words of a song, to taste a good wine, to remember where you left your keys. In one University of York study, people recalling an event they had witnessed remembered more with their eyes closed than with visual or auditory distractions in play, and the researchers concluded that closing the eyes reduces cognitive load. A kiss is simply the nicest reason you'll ever have to use the same trick.

Three More Reasons We Close Our Eyes When Kissing

Attention is the big one. Three smaller forces push in the same direction.

Your eyes can't focus that close

Every eye has a near point, the closest distance it can bring into focus. In one study of university students, it averaged about 10 centimeters (roughly 4 inches), and it was farther out in the oldest group. Mid-kiss, their face is well inside that line. Open your eyes and you get a doubled, blurry, slightly cross-eyed close-up of one eyebrow. There's nothing worth looking at, so closing them costs you nothing.

Eye contact at one inch is a lot

Across a table, holding someone's gaze is flirting. At one inch, it's an ambush. Try holding a stare at that range without laughing or looking away. Closing your eyes is the graceful exit. It lets both of you be close without being watched.

Closing your eyes lowers your guard

Your eyes are how you keep watch on the world. Shutting them in someone's arms means handing over the watch, a small act of trust. Nobody has measured this one in a lab, so treat it as my read, not a finding. It does match something I hear all the time: with someone new, or somewhere exposed, eyes stay open longer. With someone you trust, they close before you notice.

Myths About Closing Your Eyes When Kissing

Plenty of confident explanations float around this question. These are the ones I'd drop.

  • Your pupils dilate and the light hurts. You'll see this on a lot of sites. I couldn't find a single study connecting it to kissing, and people close their eyes to kiss in a dark room too.
  • Closed eyes release more oxytocin. I haven't found any research comparing hormone levels in kisses with eyes open versus closed. The real science of what a kiss does to you is interesting enough without inventing more.
  • It evolved as a signal that you're not a threat. A nice story, but nobody has tested it. It's a guess dressed up as a theory.
  • Never trust someone who kisses with their eyes open. Pure folklore, and the real reasons are usually flattering; I go through each one in what it means when someone kisses with their eyes open.

Are You Supposed to Close Your Eyes When You Kiss?

No rule says you have to. Most people close them by reflex, but I haven't found a survey I'd trust that puts a number on it, and plenty of good kissers peek.

The situations people ask me about most:

  • Your eyes close before you've even decided. Completely normal. That's the reflex doing its job.
  • Your eyes won't close, or keep popping open. Don't force it. Let them close for just the last second before contact. As you relax with this person, that second tends to stretch on its own.
  • You like to peek. Go ahead. A quick look mid-kiss costs you a little sensation and buys you the view of someone enjoying themselves. Just don't stare the whole way through; that's when it starts to feel like surveillance.
  • Quick kisses. A peck hello or a kiss on the cheek works fine with your eyes open. Plenty of people keep them open for those.

How to Use Closed Eyes to Kiss Better

If closing your eyes turns up the feeling, you can build a whole approach around it.

Watch the lean, then close for the last inch

Closing your eyes at the start of the lean is how noses collide and kisses land on chins. Watch where you're going until their face starts to blur, a few inches out, then let your eyes close for the final approach. If the nose is what worries you, here's how to angle it.

Go lighter once they're closed

The buzz in that study was easy to feel, and people still got noticeably worse at telling whether it had happened when their eyes were busy. A distracted brain can easily miss the subtle stuff in a kiss: a shift in pressure, a pause, a brush at the corner of the mouth. With your eyes closed, those small changes should come through more clearly, so give them more of them. Go lighter, vary the pressure, and slow down. Kissing slowly works so well in the dark because each small change has a better chance of being noticed.

Turn down everything else in the room

Your eyes aren't the only thing spending attention. In the York memory study, sounds hurt recall too. A buzzing phone, a TV in the background and a harsh overhead light all tug at the same budget. Before a kiss you want to land, lower the noise: phone face down, screen off, a lamp instead of the big light.

Let your hands do the seeing

With your eyes closed, your hands become your map. A palm along their jaw tells you where their face is; fingers at the back of their neck tell you when they lean in. Where your hands go matters more once sight is gone.

Say it out loud

Two words before a kiss: close your eyes. Said quietly, with a small smile, it tells them exactly what's coming and hands them the same trick you're about to use. Then make them wait one beat longer than they expect.

Open them at the end

When the kiss ends, open your eyes before you pull all the way back. You'll catch their face in the first unguarded second after a kiss, and that look tells you more about how it went than anything they'll say.

The next time your eyes fall shut mid-kiss, you'll know what's happening behind them. Nothing is shutting down. Your brain is moving everything it has to the one place it wants to be.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why do I automatically close my eyes when I kiss?

It's a reflex most people pick up without being taught, and the likeliest explanation is attention. Seeing and feeling share a limited pool of attention, and a 2016 Royal Holloway study found that a harder visual task made people worse at telling whether a brief vibration had touched their hand. Closing your eyes likely frees some of that attention for your lips, and at kissing distance your eyes can't focus anyway.

Are you supposed to close your eyes when you kiss?

There's no rule. Most people close them, probably because it lets them feel more, but peeking or keeping them open isn't wrong. If you want the kiss to feel as strong as possible, keep your eyes open for the lean and close them for the last inch.

Does closing your eyes make a kiss feel better?

It likely does. No study has tested kissing directly, but research shows the trade underneath it: when your eyes are working hard, your sense of touch gets less sharp. Closing them hands that attention to your lips, their breath and their hands.

Why does kissing with your eyes open feel weird?

At kissing distance your eyes can't focus, so you see a doubled, blurry close-up instead of a face. Your attention also splits between that blur and the kiss, and eye contact at one inch is intense. That mix is why it feels strange, even though nothing is wrong with doing it.

Do most people close their eyes when they kiss?

Most people seem to, and it's the default in films, but I haven't found a reliable survey that puts a number on it. Plenty of people peek, and some keep their eyes open out of habit. Both are normal.

When should you close your eyes during a kiss?

In the last inch or two, once you know where you're landing. Closing them at the start of the lean is how you bump noses or kiss a chin. Watch until their face starts to blur, then let your eyes close.

Is it a red flag if someone kisses with their eyes open?

No. The usual reasons are flattering: they like looking at you, they're checking how the kiss is landing, or they never picked up the habit. Judge their interest by their hands, their breathing and how they respond to you, not their eyelids. I go through every reason in my guide to kissing with your eyes open.
