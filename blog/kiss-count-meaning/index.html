Kiss Count: What It Means and What's Actually Normal

Kiss count means how many people you've kissed, not how many kisses. What surveys call normal, the numbers at 18 to 24, and what the count can't tell anyone.

The Short Answer

Your kiss count is the number of different people you've kissed romantically, not the number of kisses. Most people count lips-to-lips kisses that both people chose and skip cheek kisses, greetings and family. There's no right number: in a 2022 YouGov poll of 6,671 U.S. adults, the most common answer was five to ten people, and the middle answer among those who gave a number fell in that same range. The count says almost nothing about how well someone kisses.

Somewhere right now, someone is counting on their fingers, stalling at seven and trying to decide whether the person from the wedding afterparty counts.

If that's you, welcome. Maybe you saw "kiss count" in a comment section, maybe someone asked for yours, or maybe you just want to know where your number lands. Here's the kiss count meaning in one line: it's the number of different people you've kissed romantically. People, not kisses.

Below: what counts toward it, what real surveys say is normal (including for 18 to 24 year olds), what to say when someone asks, and a 259-year argument about kissing that Isaac Newton won. Along the way I'll make the case that your number is the least interesting thing about the way you kiss.

What Does Kiss Count Mean?

Your kiss count is the total number of different people you've kissed in a romantic or sexual way. Kiss one person a thousand times and your count goes up by one.

It's the PG cousin of "body count," the slang for how many sexual partners someone has had, and it travels the same routes: comment threads, "what's your number" videos, and the dating question that shows up somewhere between the second drink and the bill.

Urban Dictionary lists both versions, the people and the times, which is why "is kiss count people or times?" is a real search. The people version wins for a practical reason. In one survey, people in relationships reported kissing their partner about 33 times a week. At that pace, a couple passes 1,700 kisses in a year. Nobody is tallying that, and nobody should.

Kiss count measures your roster, not your reps. Hold that thought.

What Counts Toward Your Kiss Count?

There's no official scorekeeper, so here are the rules most people use:

  • Counts: a lips-to-lips kiss, romantic or flirtatious, that both of you chose.
  • Counts by most people's rules: a quick peck on the lips with someone you were into. Short still counts.
  • Doesn't count: cheek kisses, hello-and-goodbye kisses, family, and anything platonic.
  • Your call: party games, dares and stage kisses. Some people count them, some don't, and both are fine.
  • Never has to count: a kiss you didn't want. It doesn't earn a place on your list unless you decide to give it one.

Stuck on an edge case? Use what I call the Story Test. If you'd tell it as a kiss story ("so there was this one time..."), it counts. If you'd tell it as a funny story about a game, it's your call. The full rulings on cheeks, pecks and dares live in what counts as a first kiss, and the same logic works for every kiss after the first.

One more rule: the count is per person, not per relationship. Get back together with an ex and your number doesn't move. (Your group chat's opinion might.)

What's a Normal Kiss Count? What Surveys Actually Say

Let me be direct: there is no single normal number. But there is real data, and it's far calmer than the comment sections.

In July 2022, YouGov asked 6,671 U.S. adults how many people they had kissed "in a romantic or sexual way, rather than platonically." Here's how they answered:

  • 0 people: 3%
  • 1 person: 5%
  • 2 to 4 people: 19%
  • 5 to 10 people: 20%
  • 11 to 15 people: 9%
  • 16 to 20 people: 5%
  • 21 to 25 people: 4%
  • 26 or more: 12%
  • Not sure: 12%
  • Prefer not to say: 10%

A few things jump out. Five to ten was the most common answer. Line up everyone who gave a number from lowest to highest, and the person in the middle lands in that same five-to-ten range. And more than one in five adults either didn't know their number or didn't want to share it, which tells you how seriously most grown-ups take the counting.

The average versus the typical

You may have seen a bigger figure. In an anonymous online survey of 695 people described by psychologist Lucia O'Sullivan, the average number of people ever kissed was 18.6. That's a real number from a real survey, and it doesn't contradict the poll above.

Averages get dragged upward by a small group with very large counts, and that group is real: in YouGov's 2018 poll, 15% of U.S. adults said they had kissed more than 50 people. One person with a count of 150 pulls the average as hard as fifteen people with a count of ten. The two surveys asked different people, so they won't match exactly, but the pattern is a familiar one: the average runs high, and the middle tells you more about what's typical.

The 21.5 number from social media

There's also a viral claim that the average person kisses 21.5 people in their lifetime. When PinkNews looked into it in 2022, the reporter couldn't trace the study it supposedly came from. Treat it like a rumor with a decimal point.

The Average Kiss Count at 18 to 24

A lot of people searching this want the number for their own age, so here's the closest real data I could find for young adults.

In YouGov's 2018 version of the question, asked of 7,623 U.S. adults, 19% of 18 to 24 year olds said they had kissed zero people. Another 19% said two to four people, and another 19% said five to ten.

Read that again. In that age group, "nobody yet," "a few people" and "a handful" were equally common answers. There's no schedule to be behind on.

If you're curious how first kisses spread out, the average age of a first kiss shows how wide that range really is. And if your number is still zero, never been kissed is written for exactly where you are.

Does Your Kiss Count Matter?

For telling anyone how you kiss? Barely.

O'Sullivan's survey also asked what made people's best kisses great. They pointed to passion, love, and anticipation or surprise. The worst kisses failed on the qualities of the kiss itself, like too much tongue or saliva, and 44% said they had dramatically lost romantic or sexual interest in someone as a result of kissing them.

Notice what's missing from both lists: how many people the other person had kissed before.

Reps beat roster

Here's what nobody tells you about kiss count: it measures your roster, not your reps.

Skill comes from repetitions with feedback. Someone with a count of one and a long relationship has kissed the same person thousands of times, read their reactions, adjusted and been told (in words or otherwise) what lands. Someone with a count of thirty built from one-off party kisses may have written thirty first drafts and zero revisions.

Neither number is better. A big count can mean you've learned to adapt to very different styles. It can also mean you've been running the same routine on everyone and never stayed long enough to hear it wasn't working. A small count can mean depth, or just a busy few years. The number can't tell which story is yours.

What does tell? Attention. Whether you match their pace, notice when they lean in or ease off, and adjust. That's the craft behind matching your partner's kissing style, and it's learnable at any count. If you want reps without growing the roster, how to practice kissing covers what actually carries over to the real thing.

Your kiss count is a history. Your next kiss is a skill.

Kiss Count vs. Body Count: Why the Question Stings

Kiss count borrowed its shape from body count, and it borrowed a little of the baggage too.

When someone asks for your number, the question is rarely about kissing. Usually it's one of three things:

  • Comparison: "Am I behind? Am I ahead? Am I weird?"
  • Curiosity: a light, flirty way to get you telling stories.
  • Judgment: a number they plan to use to decide something about you.

The first two are human and harmless. The third is the one to notice. A person who treats your count as a verdict, too high or too low, is showing you their scorecard. It says nothing about your worth.

What to Say When Someone Asks Your Kiss Count

You never owe anyone a number. If you want to answer, here are a few ways that keep it light:

  • The honest answer: "Somewhere around eight. What's yours?" Turning it back keeps it a conversation instead of an interview.
  • The playful dodge: "Enough to know what I like." Then smile and let them wonder.
  • The story swap: "I don't keep a tally, but I'll tell you about the best one." Stories beat statistics on a date every time.
  • The boundary: "I'd rather not do numbers." Said warmly, it ends the topic without ending the evening.
  • The zero: "None yet." No apology attached. You get to decide how much more to share, and when.

If your partner's number bothers you

It happens. You hear their count, it's higher than yours or lower, and something in your stomach flips.

Ask yourself what the number would actually change. If it's higher, their past isn't a competition you're losing. If it's lower, you aren't an exam they need to pass. Either way, the only kisses that tell you anything about your relationship are the ones happening inside it.

The Other Kiss Count (Isaac Newton Had One)

Here's my favorite fact on this whole subject, and it has nothing to do with dating.

Mathematicians have their own kiss count, called the kissing number: the most identical spheres that can touch, or "kiss," one sphere of the same size without overlapping. In 1694, Isaac Newton and the astronomer David Gregory argued about it. Newton said twelve. Gregory thought a thirteenth could squeeze in.

It took until 1953 for Kurt Schütte and Bartel van der Waerden to prove Newton right. Twelve. A 259-year argument over one kiss count, settled in favor of a man who never married.

If some of history's sharpest minds needed two and a half centuries to settle a kiss count for spheres, you're allowed to stop losing sleep over yours.

The Bottom Line

Your kiss count is the number of people you've kissed, and in YouGov's 2022 poll, the most common answer among adults was five to ten. Zero, one and fifty-plus are all on the map too.

The number is a piece of history. It can't tell anyone how you kiss, how you listen or how the next kiss will go. Those parts are still being written, one rep at a time.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does kiss count mean?

Kiss count is the number of different people you've kissed romantically or sexually. It counts people, not individual kisses, so kissing the same partner many times only adds one. The term is a lighter cousin of "body count" and shows up in social media trends and dating conversations.

Is kiss count the number of people or the number of kisses?

People. Some definitions online mention both, but counting individual kisses is impossible for anyone in a relationship. People in relationships in one survey reported kissing their partner about 33 times a week, which adds up to more than 1,700 kisses a year.

What is the average kiss count?

There's no single answer, but surveys give a clear picture. In a 2022 YouGov poll of 6,671 U.S. adults, the most common answer was five to ten people, and the middle answer among those who gave a number fell in that same range. A separate survey of 695 people found an average of 18.6, a figure pulled upward by people with very high counts.

What is a normal kiss count at 18?

There isn't one normal number. In YouGov's 2018 poll, 19% of 18 to 24 year olds said they had kissed no one, 19% said two to four people and 19% said five to ten. Zero, a few and a handful were all equally common.

Does a peck count toward your kiss count?

By most people's rules, yes, if it was a romantic peck on the lips that both of you chose. Cheek kisses, greetings and kisses from family don't count. Dares and party-game kisses are up to you.

Does kiss count matter?

Not for how well someone kisses. When people describe their best and worst kisses, they talk about passion, anticipation and the quality of the kiss itself, not the other person's experience. Skill comes from attentive practice with feedback, and a long relationship with one person can provide that as well as many short ones.

Should you tell someone your kiss count?

Only if you want to. You never owe anyone a number. If you'd like to answer, keep it light, or turn it into a story swap instead of a statistic. If someone treats your number as a verdict, that tells you more about them than about you.
