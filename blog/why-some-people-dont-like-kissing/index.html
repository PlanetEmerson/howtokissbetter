Why Some People Don't Like Kissing (And What It Actually Means)

You're not broken, and you're far from alone. Here's why some people feel nothing when they kiss, what's behind it, and what to do about it.

The Short Answer

People who don't like kissing are usually reacting to something specific: the sensory side (wetness, taste, breath, a face that close), anxiety or a bad past experience, low attraction to that person, a medication or life change, or simply not being wired to crave it, which is true for some asexual people and plenty of others. It's common and it isn't a defect. To find your reason, notice whether you've never liked kissing, stopped liking it, or only dislike it with one person.

Somewhere around the third date, you realize you've been dreading the goodnight kiss more than the parking.

Not because they aren't attractive. Not because of nerves. Kissing just doesn't do anything for you, or it actively bothers you, and every romcom and love song seems to think that makes you defective. It doesn't.

This guide is for people who don't want the kiss itself. If you do want to kiss but it keeps coming out clumsy, that's a different problem with different fixes, and I cover it in why kissing feels weird.

Is It Normal to Not Like Kissing?

Yes, and it's far more common than the movies suggest.

The best numbers come from a US national survey of 1,493 people about their most recent sexual encounter, published in 2019 in the Journal of Sex & Marital Therapy. Most of them (87 percent) kissed. Among the ones who didn't, the most common reason was simply that they don't like kissing. And people under 30 who skipped it were more likely to say kissing would have been too intimate with that partner. Sex: fine. Kissing: too much.

Zoom out and kissing looks even less like a human requirement. When anthropologists looked at 168 cultures, they found evidence of romantic lip-to-lip kissing in only 46 percent of them. People fall in love, pair off and raise families all over the world without it.

So not liking kissing doesn't make you broken. It makes you someone with a preference, and preferences usually have reasons. Let's find yours.

Why Don't I Like Kissing? Find Your Pattern

Before you look at causes, answer one question: when does the dislike show up? The answer narrows things fast.

  • You've never liked it, with anyone. Start with the sensory side, then orientation (asexual or aromantic), anxiety, and plain preference. An early bad experience can live here too.
  • You used to like it and now you don't. Look at what changed around the same time: a new medication, a hormonal shift, depression or stress, or the relationship itself.
  • You like the idea, just not with this person. That points at attraction, a style mismatch, or something unresolved between you two.
  • You like some kissing, just not the wet, tongue-heavy kind. Then you don't dislike kissing. You dislike a style, and styles are negotiable. Closed-mouth kissing is a whole skill of its own (here's how to kiss without tongue), and if the real problem is spit, a too-wet kiss is fixable.

One test I like: picture kissing someone you find wildly attractive, a celebrity crush, whoever. If that sounds good and your real kisses don't, the issue is probably the person or the circumstances. If even the fantasy version sounds like a chore, it's more likely about you and kissing itself. Both answers are useful. Neither is bad news.

Why Does Kissing Gross Me Out?

Because, described literally, kissing is a lot. Another person's saliva. Their breath on your face. The taste of their last coffee. Wet little sounds right next to your ears.

Desire tends to drown all that out. Disgust and arousal seem to pull against each other: in a 2012 experiment with 90 women, those who had just watched an erotic film reported less disgust toward sex-related stimuli, and avoided them less, than women who watched a neutral or an exciting non-sexual film. The researchers note that saliva and body odor are among the strongest disgust triggers there are. Flip that around and you get the everyday version: if you aren't turned on yet, or aren't turned on by this person, someone else's saliva is just saliva.

Some nervous systems also run the volume higher. Autistic people and others with sensory processing differences can feel texture, taste, smell and light touch far more intensely. In a study of autistic adults' own accounts of sex and relationships, one person described kissing as too much texture, smell, taste and heat at once, to the point of overload. Others said light touch felt almost painful while firm touch felt good. Lips are packed with nerve endings, which is lovely when the input feels good and miserable when it doesn't.

If this is you, try:

  • Firmer and drier. A light, feathery, lingering kiss is often the worst version for a sensitive mouth. A short closed-mouth press with steady pressure is a different animal.
  • Take the wheel. When you start the kiss and end it, nothing surprises you. A hand on their jaw lets you set the pace and the distance.
  • Kiss later, not first. If arousal turns disgust down, a cold kiss at the start of the evening is the hardest version. Let it come after closeness has warmed you up.
  • Say what works. In that same study, talking to partners was the strategy autistic people mentioned most. "Short and firm is great, feathery drives me up the wall" is a perfectly attractive sentence.

Does Not Liking Kissing Mean I'm Asexual?

Not on its own. It can be part of the picture, so here's how the pieces fit.

According to the Asexual Visibility and Education Network, an asexual person doesn't experience sexual attraction. Aromantic people experience little or no romantic attraction. Both are orientations, not disorders, and both come in shades. AVEN also describes a third kind, sensual attraction: wanting non-sexual physical closeness like cuddling, hugging or kissing. Those three can show up in any combination.

That's why kissing sorts so unpredictably. Some asexual people love kissing because to them it's affectionate, not sexual. Some are indifferent. Some can't stand it. And plenty of people who feel strong sexual attraction still don't like kissing.

A more useful question than "does this make me ace?" is: do you feel drawn to people sexually, or romantically, at all? If the honest answer to either is rarely or never, and kissing is one of many things that have always felt like a script you were performing, the asexual and aromantic spectrums are worth reading about. The AVEN forums have plenty of people describing experiences like this, and finding the words can be a relief. If you do feel sexual and romantic attraction and just don't like kissing, it's most likely a preference, and only you get to decide which label, if any, fits.

Why Does Kissing Make Me Uncomfortable?

When the feeling is closer to fear than boredom, it usually comes from one of three places.

Performance anxiety. You're so busy monitoring your lips, your breath and their reaction that nothing is left over for enjoying it. This is the most fixable version, and I've written a whole guide on how to stop overthinking when you kiss.

Germ or contamination fear. Mild squeamishness about saliva is common. If the thought of someone's mouth brings intrusive thoughts, you scrub your lips afterward, or you can't stop replaying it, that can be a sign of OCD, especially if it eats up a lot of your time or feels out of your control. Fear of germs or contamination is a common OCD obsession, and a doctor or therapist can tell you whether that's what's going on. It's treatable; the NIMH highlights a form of CBT called exposure and response prevention.

A past experience your body remembers. A forced kiss, pressure you didn't want, or abuse can change how touch registers long after your mind has filed it away. In a 2019 study, people with PTSD from interpersonal trauma strongly disliked slow skin-to-skin stroking that the comparison group enjoyed, while their ratings of impersonal touch didn't differ. If kissing brings panic, nausea, freezing or a strange blank numbness, that can be your nervous system trying to protect you, not a character flaw. A trauma-informed therapist can help at your pace, and nobody is owed a kiss while you work it out.

Why Did I Stop Liking Kissing?

If you used to enjoy kissing and now you'd rather not, something changed. Look at what else changed around the same time.

Medication. Antidepressants are a common culprit. In a survey of 669 people taking antidepressants for depression, 46 percent reported emotional blunting, a flattening of good feelings along with the bad, though the researchers note depression itself plays a part. Some medicines also dry your mouth: the NIDCR names drugs for high blood pressure, depression and bladder control as common culprits, and a dry mouth makes kissing feel sticky and wrong.

Hormones, stress and life. The NHS list of causes of low sex drive doubles as a timeline check: relationship problems, stress, anxiety or depression, pregnancy and a new baby, menopause, hormonal contraception, certain medicines, too much alcohol. Kissing tends to fade along with desire in general.

You stopped performing. Some people spend years kissing because they were supposed to. Older and more honest with yourself, you finally notice you never loved it. That isn't a loss. It's accuracy.

If the timing lines up with a new prescription, bring it up with whoever prescribed it rather than stopping on your own. There are often alternatives.

Why Don't I Like Kissing My Partner?

This one stings, because you love them. Work through it in order.

  • Is it them, or kissing? Run the crush test from earlier. If kissing someone else appeals and kissing your partner doesn't, keep going.
  • Is it their style? Too wet, too hard, too fast, tongue first. That's a mismatch, not a verdict on the relationship, and one honest conversation can fix it. Here's how to tell someone they're a bad kisser without wrecking their week.
  • Is it hygiene or health? Breath, smoking, a dental problem, a medication on their side. Awkward to raise, easy to fix.
  • Is it the relationship? Resentment, a fight that never got resolved, feeling unseen. Bodies often pull away from the mouth before the mind admits anything is wrong. If that sounds like you two, why couples stop kissing covers how to bring it back.
  • Is the attraction gone? Sometimes you love someone and aren't drawn to them physically, or never were. That's hard to admit. It's still worth knowing, because it calls for a very different conversation than "could you kiss me more gently?"

Can You Learn to Like Kissing?

Sometimes. It depends on your pattern.

If the problem is style, sensory overload or nerves, often yes. Try this for two or three weeks with someone you trust:

  • You start the kiss, you end it, you choose how long it lasts.
  • Keep it short, dry and closed-mouthed until that feels easy, then add one thing at a time.
  • Kiss when you're already relaxed and close, not as the opening move.
  • Agree on a signal, a word or a squeeze, that means pause, with no hurt feelings attached.
  • Afterward, talk about what worked, not only what didn't.

If it's orientation or a settled preference, you probably won't learn to love it, and you don't need to. Forcing yourself can backfire, because your body learns that kissing means gritting your teeth.

Get help instead of pushing through if it causes you real distress, if it started suddenly, if it comes with panic or intrusive thoughts, or if trauma is involved. Depending on your pattern, that means a sex therapist, a trauma-informed therapist, or your doctor.

How to Tell Your Partner You Don't Like Kissing

Your partner has probably noticed already. Without an explanation, they'll write the worst version themselves: you're not attracted to them, you're pulling away, it's over. The truth is almost always kinder than the story they're telling themselves.

Lead with what you do want, give a reason if you have one, and offer something to move toward. Some words you can borrow:

  • Early dating: "I really like you, and I'm slow to warm up to kissing. It isn't you. Holding hands and being close is more my speed for now."
  • Long-term, and it's always been this way: "I love being close to you. Kissing has never really been my thing, and that's not about you. Can we figure out together what feels good for both of us?"
  • Long-term, and it changed: "I've noticed I'm avoiding kissing, and I don't want you to think it's you. I think it started around the new medication. Can we talk about it?"
  • Sensory reasons: "Wet, light kisses overload me. Short and firm works much better. Want to try it that way?"

Then let them react. They may feel hurt, relieved, or both. You can make room for their feelings and still keep your boundary.

If kissing matters a lot to them and very little to you, treat it like any other mismatch in desire: talk about it openly and look for the overlap. Many couples find it in the kisses that do work: forehead, cheek, a quick peck at the door. What doesn't work is years of quiet dread on one side and quiet rejection on the other.

The best kiss is the one both people actually want to be having. And if that kiss lands on the forehead instead of the mouth, it still counts.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you be in love and not like kissing?

Yes. Love, attraction and liking one specific physical act are separate things, and plenty of people deeply love a partner while finding kissing uncomfortable or dull. What matters is that you both know, and that you find other ways to feel close.

Can a relationship work without kissing?

Yes, if both people are truly fine with it. Relationships run on closeness and honesty more than on any single act, and many couples swap mouth kissing for cuddling, forehead kisses or other touch. It gets hard when one partner quietly misses it, so say it out loud instead of hoping nobody notices.

Why do I like sex but not kissing?

You're far from the only one. In a 2019 US national survey, people under 30 who skipped kissing the last time they had sex were more likely to say it would have been too intimate with that partner. Kissing is face to face with nowhere to hide, which can feel more exposing than sex, and sensory discomfort can be stronger at the mouth than anywhere else.

Why do I feel nothing when I kiss someone?

Usually because something a kiss needs is missing: attraction to that person, feeling relaxed and safe, or a bit of anticipation. Nerves, low desire, medication and a simple mismatch can all flatten it. If it's flat with one person, it's probably the match; if it's flat with everyone, every time, work through the patterns above.

Is there a phobia of kissing?

Yes, informally called philematophobia or philemaphobia. It isn't a separate diagnosis, but a fear of kissing intense enough to cause panic or avoidance can count as a phobia, and a doctor or therapist can help you work out whether it does. Phobias can be treated, often with a talking therapy such as CBT.

Is it a red flag if my partner doesn't like kissing?

Not by itself. Plenty of people who adore their partners don't enjoy kissing, for sensory, emotional or orientation reasons. It's worth a conversation if it comes with pulling away from all touch and closeness, and the way to find out is to ask kindly, without accusing.
